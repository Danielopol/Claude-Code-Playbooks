## What This Does

Explains any digital artifact — a paper, PDF, article, code diff, or whole repo — through a persistent profile of how you personally learn, then checks that the explanation actually landed.

Three things make it different from asking for a summary. It **interviews you first** (about ten minutes, once) and writes a `LEARNER.md` capturing what you already know deeply, how you like to enter a topic, what convinces you, and what understanding is *for* in your life. It **quizzes you** at the end of every explanation, grading your free-text answers itself rather than letting you self-grade. And every question becomes a **spaced-repetition card**, so what you understood in July is still there in October.

The design goal it states outright: formation, not summarization. Not just what you can do with an artifact, but who you become by understanding it.

## The Explanation Spine

Every explanation follows the same five-part shape, with the profile read first:

1. **Why does this exist?** — the problem the artifact answers. Context before content.
2. **The one idea** — the single sentence worth retaining above all others.
3. **How it works** — intuition before mechanism, using analogies pulled from domains you already know, skipping what your profile says you already understand.
4. **What can you now do** — pointed at your stated goals: what you can build, argue, or decide that you couldn't before.
5. **Check yourself** — the quiz.

A depth dial controls how far it goes: `orient` (a five-minute pass), `working` (you could explain it to a colleague, the default), or `rederivable` (you could rebuild it). Say "just orient me" or "I want to really grok this" to override per request.

## Adapters by Artifact Type

Each artifact type gets its own definition of what "meaning" even means:

- **Papers and PDFs** — claim plus evidence. Reads in evidence order (abstract, figures, results, methods) rather than page order, and treats the gap between what the authors *measured* and what they *claim* as the high-value thing to explain.
- **Articles and essays** — the argument arc. Names the implied opponent, separates what's argued from what's merely asserted, and flags the weakest link honestly.
- **Code, diffs, and repos** — behavioral change and intent. Follows one path through the system in narrative order instead of reading files alphabetically, and flags which parts are the idea versus which are plumbing.

## Why the Quizzes Are Unusual

Most AI-generated quizzes test whether you remember the explanation you just read. This one explicitly forbids that. Questions are drafted **from the artifact, not the explanation**, then self-checked with an echo test: if the answer appears near-verbatim in the explanation, the question gets cut for measuring short-term verbal memory rather than understanding.

The other rules worth knowing:

- Every question must be **load-bearing** — if missing it wouldn't change what you can do with the artifact, it's trivia and gets cut. No author names, no years, no constants.
- Explain-back questions are graded on exactly three criteria: core claim present, mechanism correct, **nothing invented**. That last one is the anti-confabulation check.
- On a miss, it re-explains the missed idea **differently** rather than repeating itself. That's the first learning moment, before the card ever enters the deck.
- When grading is ambiguous between hit and partial, it's instructed to choose partial, because it wrote the explanation *and* grades the quiz and knows that biases it.

## The Evidence-Gated Profile

The part that separates this from a profile that just accumulates opinions about you: it can only claim a weakness it can prove. A "Known Weak Pattern" gets written to your profile only after **4+ misses on the same tag across at least 2 different artifacts** within 60 days. The cross-artifact requirement is what distinguishes "struggles with derivations" from "that one paper was brutal."

Every entry cites its evidence inline (`[evidence: 5 misses / 3 artifacts, Jul–Sep]`), auditable against the raw signal log. Patterns also **heal** — once your hit rate on a tag recovers past 80%, the entry becomes eligible for retirement, because a deficit-only profile turns into a self-fulfilling ceiling. If a question was simply bad, you say so and the signal is excluded, so generation failures never become facts about you.

## Tips

- Answer interview question 6 ("what are you trying to build or become this year?") properly. It's the one that turns explanations into something pointed at your goals rather than generic teaching.
- Non-work domains make the best analogies. When it asks what you know well enough to teach, include the hobby, not just the job.
- Your `LEARNER.md` is yours to edit. If an entry about your weaknesses feels wrong, delete it — the skill is built to expect that.
- The stretch policy is deliberate: roughly one explanation in five leads with an unfamiliar frame, flagged as such, so comfort-only explanations don't become a ceiling.

## Limitations

- **This packages a multi-file skill as one CLAUDE.md.** The original ships helper scripts (`sm2.ts` for card scheduling, `scan-signals.ts` for the threshold and heal scans) that a single file can't include. The template documents every file format and threshold so Claude can maintain the deck directly, but installing the [full bundle](https://github.com/DannyMac180/skills/tree/main/explain-this) is less error-prone if you plan to use review mode heavily.
- The first run costs about ten minutes before you get any explanation. That's the design, not a bug, but it's real friction if you just wanted one paper explained.
- State lives in `~/.explain-this/`. Back it up — the source calls losing a learner's history the worst failure the tool can have.
- Spaced repetition only pays off if you actually run the reviews. The skill nudges once when cards are due and is explicitly forbidden from nagging, so the discipline is yours.
