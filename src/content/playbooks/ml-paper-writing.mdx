## What This Does

A full-pipeline guide for drafting ML/AI conference papers, anchored on two disciplines most AI-assisted paper-writing skips: citation integrity and evidence-backed claims. Every citation goes through a mandatory programmatic verification workflow — search Semantic Scholar/CrossRef/arXiv/OpenAlex, confirm the metadata, fetch the BibTeX from the DOI (never typed from memory), and verify the specific claim being cited actually appears in the source paper — with an explicit, unambiguous rule for what happens when verification fails: mark it `[CITATION NEEDED]` and tell the scientist, never invent a plausible-sounding reference. A parallel "claim ledger gate" applies the same discipline to the paper's own content: before a project plan, experiment note, or literature summary becomes manuscript prose, its supporting claim or evidence record gets identified explicitly, and anything that can't be traced gets marked `[CLAIM NEEDS EVIDENCE]` rather than smoothed into confident-sounding prose.

Beyond integrity, it synthesizes sentence-level and structural writing guidance from researchers who've published extensively at top ML venues — Neel Nanda's advice to spend roughly equal time on the abstract, introduction, and figures as on everything else combined (since reviewers read title → abstract → intro → figures → maybe the rest, and rarely reach a poorly-hooked methods section), Gopen & Swan's seven reader-expectation principles (subject-verb proximity, stress position, old-before-new information ordering) with concrete before/after rewrites, and Zachary Lipton's word-choice heuristics (drop hedging, avoid incremental-sounding verbs like "combine" or "modify" when the contribution is genuinely novel). It closes with practical mechanics: a conference requirements quick-reference (with an explicit note to verify exact current-year limits, since these change annually) and a template-handling checklist that insists on copying the entire venue template directory and verifying it compiles before writing a word of content.


## Tips & Best Practices

- Never accept a citation the workflow couldn't verify — treat a `[CITATION NEEDED]` or `[PLACEHOLDER - VERIFY]` marker as a hard stop requiring your own manual check, not a formatting detail to clean up later.
- Apply the equal-time-on-abstract/intro/figures allocation literally, especially under deadline pressure — it's tempting to rush the abstract to get to the "real" methods writing, which is backwards from how reviewers actually read.
- Delete an abstract's first sentence if it could be prepended unchanged to any other ML paper in the field — that's the fastest single diagnostic for a generic opening that needs the actual contribution stated instead.

## Limitations

- The citation-verification workflow depends on access to programmatic literature-search APIs (Semantic Scholar, CrossRef, arXiv, OpenAlex); without that access, citation checking falls back to slower, less reliable manual verification and should be flagged as such.
- Conference page limits and specific requirements (checklists, disclosure statements, style files) change year to year — always confirm current numbers against the venue's own current call for papers rather than trusting a cached figure.
- Writing-philosophy guidance is synthesized specifically for ML/AI conference papers; a journal submission, a theory-heavy math paper, or a systems paper with different reviewer norms may need different structural conventions.
