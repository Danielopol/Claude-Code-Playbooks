## What This Does

A guide to implementing the saga pattern — the standard way to run a multi-step business process across services when a single ACID transaction isn't possible. It lays out the two coordination styles and when each fits (choreography, where services react to each other's events, for short flows; orchestration, where a central coordinator explicitly drives every step and its undo, once a saga grows past a few steps), with an explicit state model (Started → Pending → Compensating → Completed/Failed). The core is a step-plus-compensation model: every step that has a side effect defines the compensating action that reverses it, and on failure the orchestrator runs the completed steps' compensations in reverse order rather than leaving the system in a half-done state.

It covers the mechanics that make a saga production-grade: persisting saga state after each transition so a crashed orchestrator resumes where it stopped (sagas are long-running and must survive restarts), per-step timeouts so a stuck step compensates instead of hanging forever, a worked order-fulfillment example (reserve inventory → charge payment → ship, each with its compensation), and the choreography variant with correlation IDs for tracing a flow that has no central coordinator. The do's and don'ts target the parts teams get wrong: non-idempotent steps that break on retry, compensations that don't actually undo, synchronous service coupling, and — most of all — shipping the rollback path untested, since it's the most critical and least-exercised code in the whole saga.

## Tips & Best Practices

- Write and test every compensation as carefully as its forward action — the rollback path is the entire point of a saga and the part that's almost never exercised in normal operation, so it's where latent bugs hide until the worst moment.
- Make each step idempotent from the start; the orchestrator retries on transient failures, and a step that double-charges or double-ships on a retry is a far worse failure than the one it was recovering from.
- Persist saga state on every transition — treating the saga as in-memory state that a restart can lose turns a recoverable partial failure into an orphaned, half-completed workflow.

## Limitations

- Sagas provide eventual consistency and atomicity-via-compensation, not true isolation — intermediate states are visible to other readers, so a flow that genuinely needs isolation needs a different approach.
- The patterns are framework-agnostic Python illustrations; wiring them to your actual message bus, saga store, and service APIs is real implementation work the playbook guides but doesn't do for you.
- Choreography can become hard to reason about as steps multiply; the playbook recommends orchestration past a few steps, but that tradeoff still depends on your team's tolerance for a central coordinator vs. distributed event flow.
