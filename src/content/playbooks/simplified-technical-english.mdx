## What This Does

Applies ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English — the controlled-language standard aerospace and defense manufacturers use for maintenance documentation — to software docs, runbooks, error messages, release notes, incident reports, and agent instructions. Every passage first gets classified as procedural (tells the reader what to do, 20-word sentence cap, imperative mood) or descriptive (explains what something is, 25-word cap, no imperative), then the structural rules apply: one instruction per sentence, condition before command, banned soft modals (should/would/may/might/could all collapse to must/can or get deleted), and one consistent word per concept throughout the document.

Because the rules exist to protect a tired, possibly non-native reader from misreading an instruction, they double as an effective de-slopping pass — a companion table maps the specific words AI-generated docs overuse (leverage, seamlessly, robust, facilitate, gracefully handles) to plain replacements or outright deletion, while code, identifiers, CLI flags, and quoted error text stay untouched as the technical names they are.

```bash

```

Paste in the documentation, error message, or runbook you want rewritten and ask Claude to apply the Simplified Technical English rules. For a compliance check instead of a rewrite, ask it to report each violation by rule with a compliant rewrite.

## Tips & Best Practices

- Run the self-check before delivering, every time: count your three longest sentences, search for contractions and any surviving "should," and confirm every "if"/"when" opens its sentence before the command it gates.
- The "should" ban matters more for agent-facing instructions (system prompts, CLAUDE.md) than almost anywhere else — a model reads "should" as optional even when a human author meant it as a hard requirement.
- Don't apply this to marketing copy or brand voice — it strips persuasion by design. If asked for STE on marketing text, say so and offer it for the surrounding docs instead.

## Limitations

- An independent, unofficial application of a public standard's structural rules — not an official ASD/STEMG tool, and no automated pass can guarantee full compliance with the actual ASD-STE100 dictionary.
- Full vocabulary compliance (the ~900 approved words) requires the official ASD dictionary, a free download at asd-ste100.org — this playbook covers the structural rules, which get most of the clarity benefit without it.
- Best suited to technical, instructional, and safety-relevant text — applying it to narrative or persuasive writing actively works against the writer's goals.
